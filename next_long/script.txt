Tonight, the Moon is about 384,000 kilometers away. That is 239,000 miles. It has been drifting away from us, slowly, for billions of years.
Now imagine it stops moving. Not the light. Not the glow. Just its speed.
The Moon is not floating up there. It is falling toward us every second. It only misses because it is moving sideways at about one kilometer per second. Take that speed away, and nothing is left to make it miss.
This is a thought experiment. No known force could stop the Moon like this. But following it through shows how much of your world depends on a rock you hardly notice.
This is what happens when the Moon falls toward Earth. The first hours. The last day. And the moment the Moon stops being a moon.
Picture it. It is night. You are standing on a beach. The Moon is full, and it looks exactly the way it always has.
For the first few hours, nothing seems wrong. The Moon starts its fall slowly, the way anything does when it is let go. Scientists can work out how long the whole fall would take. The answer is about four days and nineteen hours.
Day one. The change is too small to see with your eyes. But instruments would see it. Laser beams bounced off mirrors that astronauts left on the Moon would show the distance shrinking, faster every hour.
Day two. The nights get brighter. A full Moon already lights up a field well enough to walk across. As it closes in, moonlight grows stronger, until night stops feeling like night.
And the fall is not one-sided. The Moon pulls on Earth too. Both bodies fall toward a shared point between them. Earth is about eighty times heavier, so that point sits inside our planet. But Earth still shifts toward the Moon as it comes.
But the Moon is not just a light in the sky. It pulls on the oceans. That pull is what makes the tides.
And tidal pull grows very fast as the Moon gets closer. Halve the distance, and the tides become about eight times stronger.
Day three. Eclipses stop being rare. The Moon grows large enough to cover the Sun easily, and its shadow sweeps across a much bigger part of the planet. Whole regions go dark in the middle of the day, again and again.
Could anything stop it? No rocket, no explosion, nothing humans have ever built comes close. The Moon has more than seventy billion billion tonnes of mass. Everyone on Earth would simply have to watch.
By the end of the third day, the Moon is about half as far away as it was. It looks twice as wide. It covers four times as much of the sky. And the sea in front of you no longer behaves like a sea.
High tide is no longer a slow rise. Coastal cities see the water climb far past any flood line they have ever recorded, then pull back miles out to sea, twice a day.
The rock under your feet feels it too. The Moon pulls on the ground, not just the water. Earth's crust flexes, and the stress builds along every fault line.
Scientists would expect earthquakes and volcanic eruptions to increase as the pull grows. Exactly how many, and where, is uncertain. The direction is not.
Day four. The Moon is now close enough that its gravity is no longer gentle. It is tearing at the planet. And the planet is tearing back.
Because here is the part almost no one expects. The Moon never hits the Earth. Not as one piece.
Every large object close to a planet has a line it cannot cross and stay whole. It is called the Roche limit. Inside it, the planet's pull on the near side of the moon is so much stronger than on the far side that the moon is stretched apart.
For our Moon, that line sits somewhere around eighteen thousand kilometers, or about eleven thousand miles, from Earth. The exact figure depends on how strong the Moon's rock is. But the result is the same.
The Moon cracks. Then it stretches. Then it comes apart into a stream of rubble the size of mountains, wrapping itself around the planet.
For a brief moment, Earth would have something like the rings of Saturn. A glowing band of broken Moon across the whole sky.
It would be the most beautiful thing anyone has ever seen. And it would not last.
Long before the big pieces land, the space around Earth becomes a shooting gallery. Satellites, the space station, the GPS network, every machine in orbit sits in the path of that rubble. One by one, they go dark.
Those fragments are still falling. They spread out, collide, and spiral in. The biggest pieces strike the Earth one after another, each one releasing more energy than every weapon ever built.
The sky fills with debris. Rock vaporizes on impact and spreads around the planet. Scientists would expect the heat from that much material falling at once to set the surface on fire, and possibly melt large parts of the crust.
The oceans would boil away. The atmosphere would fill with vaporized rock. Life on the surface would not survive.
Meanwhile, the land and the sea lose their oldest rhythm. No tides. No moonlight. Animals that time their lives to the Moon, from coral spawning to sea turtle nesting, lose their clock.
There is a quieter loss too. The Moon helps steady the tilt of Earth's axis. That tilt is what gives you seasons. Without a large moon, some scientists think the tilt could wander much more over millions of years, and climates with it.
But here is the strangest part of the story. This has happened before. In reverse.
Most scientists think the Moon was born in a collision. About four and a half billion years ago, a planet roughly the size of Mars, often called Theia, struck the young Earth.
The impact threw a ring of molten debris into orbit. Over time, that ring pulled itself together into the Moon.
So if the Moon fell, it would not be inventing a new kind of disaster. It would be undoing the one that made it. Ring to Moon, billions of years ago. Moon to ring, in less than five days.
So, could any of this happen? No. The Moon is not falling toward us. It is moving away, about three point eight centimeters, or one and a half inches, every year.
That is roughly the speed your fingernails grow. Over billions of years, it adds up. The Moon will keep drifting, the tides will slowly weaken, and the day will keep getting longer.
Nothing is coming to stop it. Not in any time that matters to us.
Go back to that beach. The full Moon over the water. The tide coming in, right on time.
That steady rhythm, the water rising and falling twice a day, has been with this planet for longer than life has. It is held in place by one thing. The Moon is falling, all the time. It just never stops missing.
